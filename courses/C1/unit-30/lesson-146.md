# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- self-awareness – capacity to recognise oneself as a distinct individual with thoughts, emotions and an identity
- sentience – capacity to experience sensations, feelings and subjective states
- symbolic thought – ability to represent ideas through symbols such as language, numbers, images or abstract concepts
- moral cognition – mental processes involved in evaluating actions as right, wrong, fair or unfair
- collective intelligence – ability of groups to solve problems or create knowledge through cooperation
- cultural transmission – process through which knowledge, beliefs and behaviour are passed from one generation to another
- capacity for abstraction – ability to think beyond immediate physical reality and reason about ideas, possibilities or concepts
- prosocial behaviour – voluntary behaviour intended to benefit other people or society
- social interconnectedness – condition of being deeply dependent on relationships and networks with other people
- human distinctiveness – characteristics believed to differentiate humans from other animals or intelligent systems

# 6. Reading – More Than Intelligence

Read.

Humans have often defined themselves through intelligence. Our ability to use complex language, develop technology and manipulate the environment appears to separate us from other species. Yet many qualities once considered uniquely human have gradually been identified elsewhere in the animal world.

Some animals use tools, cooperate, communicate complex information and display behaviour resembling empathy or grief. This does not erase the differences between humans and other species, but it complicates attempts to define humanity through a single ability.

One important distinction may lie in accumulation. Humans do not merely learn individually; they inherit enormous bodies of knowledge from previous generations. Language, institutions, technology and cultural practices allow knowledge to accumulate over centuries rather than disappearing with each individual.

Another possible distinction is the ability to construct shared imagined realities. Money, laws, nations and institutions exist partly because large groups of people collectively accept symbolic systems and behave as though they are real. These abstractions enable cooperation on a scale rarely observed elsewhere.

Humanity may therefore be less about possessing one unique characteristic and more about the interaction between cognition, culture, emotion and extreme social interconnectedness.
