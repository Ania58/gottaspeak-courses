# 2. Language for Nuanced Argument

At C1 level, strong argumentation rarely means presenting an issue as completely right or completely wrong.

Use these expressions to develop more nuanced positions.

- **on balance** – after considering the different arguments or factors
- **to a large extent** – mostly, although not completely
- **there is a compelling case for...** – there are strong reasons supporting a particular position
- **that interpretation overlooks...** – introduces an important factor missing from an argument
- **the distinction is not clear-cut** – the difference between two categories is more complicated than it appears
- **this raises a broader question about...** – connects a specific issue to a larger problem
- **there is an inherent tension between...** – two desirable ideas or goals naturally come into conflict
- **a more nuanced view would be...** – introduces a position that recognises greater complexity
- **the argument holds up only if...** – identifies a condition necessary for an argument to remain convincing
- **ultimately, it comes down to...** – identifies what you consider the central issue after considering other factors

# 6. Reading – The Problem with Simple Answers

Read.

Human beings are remarkably good at simplifying complicated realities. We divide people into good and bad, decisions into right and wrong, societies into successful and unsuccessful, and lives into meaningful and wasted. Such categories are useful because they allow us to navigate a world containing far more information than we could ever process individually.

Yet intellectual maturity often involves becoming less comfortable with simple answers.

A policy may increase freedom while simultaneously increasing inequality. A technological innovation may solve one problem while creating another. A person's harmful behaviour may be both understandable and morally unacceptable. An experience may be painful while ultimately becoming valuable. Two apparently contradictory claims may even contain elements of truth.

Recognising complexity does not require abandoning judgement. If every question were answered with "it depends", critical thinking would become an excuse for avoiding conclusions. The challenge is therefore to make decisions while remaining aware of what those decisions fail to capture.

Perhaps wisdom lies partly in maintaining this tension: having convictions without assuming infallibility, making choices without pretending that alternatives have no value, and accepting uncertainty without becoming paralysed by it.

The ability to say "I may be wrong" is not necessarily evidence of weak thinking. Sometimes it is evidence that thinking has genuinely taken place.
