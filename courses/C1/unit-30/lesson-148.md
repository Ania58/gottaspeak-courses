# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- human augmentation – use of technology or biological intervention to extend human physical or cognitive capabilities
- cognitive enhancement – improvement of abilities such as memory, attention, learning or reasoning beyond an individual's previous level
- biotechnological intervention – deliberate modification of biological processes through medical or technological methods
- human-machine integration – incorporation of technological systems into human biological or cognitive functioning
- transhumanism – movement advocating the use of technology to overcome significant human biological limitations
- biological limitation – constraint resulting from the natural capacities of the human body or mind
- identity continuity – degree to which a person remains recognisably the same individual despite physical, psychological or technological change
- enhancement inequality – social inequality resulting from unequal access to technologies that improve human abilities
- genetic modification – deliberate alteration of genetic material in order to change particular biological characteristics
- post-human condition – hypothetical future state in which technological transformation has altered human beings so extensively that existing definitions of humanity may no longer apply

# 6. Reading – From Repairing Humans to Redesigning Them

Read.

Human beings have always used technology to overcome biological limitations. Glasses compensate for imperfect eyesight, vaccines strengthen our resistance to disease, and artificial limbs can restore abilities lost through injury. Few people regard such interventions as threats to human identity.

The ethical questions become more complicated when technology moves beyond restoration. Imagine an artificial eye that does not merely restore ordinary sight but enables its user to see infrared light. Or consider a neurological implant that gives a healthy person a dramatically improved memory. Such interventions would not simply return individuals to a typical range of human ability; they would extend that range.

Supporters of human enhancement argue that the distinction between treatment and improvement is less obvious than it first appears. Education enhances cognitive abilities. Exercise improves the body. Caffeine alters alertness. Humans already modify themselves constantly; biotechnology may simply provide more powerful tools for doing so.

Critics respond that the scale and permanence of future interventions could make them fundamentally different. If wealthier individuals could purchase superior memory, intelligence or physical abilities, existing inequalities might become biological as well as economic. Enhancement could also create social pressure. Once a technology became common, refusing it might cease to be a meaningful choice if unenhanced individuals could no longer compete.

The deepest question concerns human identity. If technology eventually allows us to redesign intelligence, emotion, ageing and the body itself, we may need to decide whether being human depends on particular biological characteristics or on something more fundamental that could survive their transformation.
