# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- determinism – view that events, including human actions, arise from prior causes and conditions
- causal chain – sequence of events in which each event contributes to producing the next
- behavioural predisposition – increased tendency to behave in a particular way because of biological, psychological or environmental factors
- volitional control – capacity to deliberately regulate one's actions according to conscious intentions
- moral agency – ability to make decisions that can appropriately be evaluated in moral terms
- circumstantial constraint – external condition that limits the realistic choices available to a person
- conscious deliberation – intentional process of considering alternatives before making a decision
- individual accountability – responsibility assigned to a person for their own actions and consequences
- compatibilism – philosophical position that meaningful free will can exist even if human behaviour has causes
- causal determinacy – condition in which an outcome is fully determined by preceding factors

# 6. Reading – Are We the Authors of Our Choices?

Read.

People generally experience themselves as agents. We consider alternatives, make decisions and feel that we could have acted differently. This experience forms the basis of many social institutions: praise, blame, punishment and responsibility all appear to assume that individuals exercise meaningful control over their behaviour.

Yet every decision emerges from conditions that the individual did not choose. Nobody chooses their genes, early childhood, temperament, native culture or many of the experiences that shape later preferences. Even conscious decisions are influenced by emotional states, habits and circumstances that may operate partly outside awareness.

This tension lies at the heart of the debate about free will. Determinists argue that if every decision arises from prior causes, then the idea that a person could genuinely have acted otherwise becomes difficult to defend. Critics respond that causation does not automatically eliminate agency. A decision can have causes and still express the person's values, reasoning and intentions.

This latter position is associated with compatibilism. According to this view, freedom does not require actions to appear from nowhere without causes. Instead, a person acts freely when they are able to respond to reasons, reflect on alternatives and behave according to their own motivations without excessive external coercion.

The debate therefore depends partly on what we expect free will to mean. If freedom requires complete independence from all prior causes, it may be impossible. If it means the capacity for conscious deliberation and self-control within real-world constraints, meaningful agency may still exist.

# 16. Three Philosophical Positions

Compare these positions.

## Position A – Hard Determinism

Every human decision is the result of preceding causes.

Traditional free will does not exist.

## Position B – Libertarian Free Will

People sometimes genuinely originate choices that are not fully determined by previous causes.

## Position C – Compatibilism

Our decisions have causes, but we can still be meaningfully free when we act according to our own reasoning and motivations without coercion.

For each position, identify:

- its strongest argument
- its biggest weakness
- what it implies about responsibility
