# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- demographic transition – long-term shift in population patterns, particularly birth rates, death rates and age structure
- technological convergence – merging of different technologies in ways that create new capabilities or applications
- societal transformation – fundamental change in how a society is organised or functions
- existential risk – threat capable of permanently destroying humanity or severely limiting its future potential
- human augmentation – use of technology to enhance human physical or cognitive abilities beyond ordinary biological limits
- intergenerational responsibility – obligation to consider how present actions will affect people living in the future
- long-term trajectory – general direction in which a society, technology or system develops over an extended period
- future-proofing – designing systems so that they can remain useful and resilient despite future changes
- demographic imbalance – substantial difference between population groups, often involving age or geographical distribution
- path dependency – situation in which decisions made in the past restrict or strongly influence the options available in the future

# 6. Reading

Read.

Predictions about the distant future often reveal as much about the present as they do about what is actually likely to happen. Each generation tends to imagine the future by extending the technologies, fears and ambitions of its own time. As a result, many predictions eventually appear surprisingly inaccurate.

Nevertheless, thinking about the distant future serves an important purpose. Decisions concerning infrastructure, energy, artificial intelligence and environmental protection may create consequences lasting for decades or even centuries. Some choices also produce path dependency: once societies invest heavily in particular technologies or systems, changing direction can become extremely difficult.

This creates a problem of representation. Future generations will live with the consequences of decisions made today, yet they cannot participate in those decisions. Governments and businesses also tend to operate within relatively short time horizons, while many global challenges require planning across generations.

Thinking seriously about the future therefore does not require predicting exactly what will happen. Instead, it requires considering a range of plausible possibilities and ensuring that today's decisions do not unnecessarily restrict the choices available to those who come after us.
