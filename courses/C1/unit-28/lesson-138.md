# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- resource depletion – reduction of a natural resource because it is being consumed faster than it can be replenished
- ecological overshoot – situation in which human demand on ecosystems exceeds their capacity to regenerate resources
- supply-chain vulnerability – susceptibility of production and distribution networks to disruption
- resource-intensive – requiring particularly large quantities of energy, water, raw materials or other resources
- scarcity-induced conflict – dispute or violence intensified by competition over limited resources
- extraction pressure – increasing demand to remove natural resources from the environment
- resource substitution – replacement of a scarce material with a more abundant or sustainable alternative
- strategic resource – material considered particularly important for a country's economy, security or technological development
- regenerative capacity – ability of a natural system to replace resources and recover after use or damage
- material footprint – total quantity of raw materials required to support an individual's, organisation's or country's consumption

# 6. Reading

Read.

Discussions about resource scarcity often create the impression that humanity is simply approaching the point at which essential materials will disappear. In reality, scarcity is considerably more complicated. A resource may remain physically abundant while becoming difficult or expensive to extract, politically inaccessible or unevenly distributed.

Technology can also change what counts as a scarce resource. New extraction methods may increase available supplies, while recycling and substitution can reduce dependence on particular materials. At the same time, technological development can create entirely new forms of demand. The rapid expansion of batteries, renewable-energy infrastructure and digital technologies, for example, has increased the strategic importance of minerals that previously attracted relatively little public attention.

Consumption therefore matters as much as physical availability. If demand continues to grow indefinitely, improvements in efficiency may merely delay rather than solve the underlying problem. This is particularly important for renewable resources such as forests, fish stocks and freshwater systems. They can regenerate, but only if human consumption remains within their regenerative capacity.

The challenge is consequently not simply to discover more resources. It is to decide how resources should be extracted, distributed, consumed and reused in a world where both population and material expectations continue to change.

# 9. The Rebound Effect

Imagine a new technology makes household appliances **50% more energy-efficient**.

Because they are now cheaper to operate, people:

- use them more frequently
- buy more appliances
- replace older devices more quickly

As a result, total energy consumption falls by only 10%.

Discuss.

1. Does technological efficiency necessarily reduce consumption?
2. Why might people consume more when something becomes cheaper or more efficient?
3. Should environmental policy focus on efficiency or absolute consumption?
4. Can economic growth continue indefinitely while resource consumption falls?

# 10. A Circular Economy

A traditional model of production often looks like this:

**extract → manufacture → use → discard**

A circular economy attempts to keep materials in use for as long as possible:

**design → use → repair → reuse → recycle**

Consider:

- clothes
- smartphones
- furniture
- cars
- household appliances

For each product, suggest one change that would make its life cycle more circular.

Then discuss:

1. Which industries could realistically become highly circular?
2. Which materials are difficult to reuse indefinitely?
3. Should companies remain responsible for products after selling them?
4. Would consumers accept higher prices for products designed to last much longer?
