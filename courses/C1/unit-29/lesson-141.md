# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- subjective well-being – an individual's personal evaluation of how positively or negatively they experience their life
- life satisfaction – cognitive assessment of the overall quality of one's life rather than a temporary emotional state
- psychological fulfilment – deeper sense that one's needs, abilities and values are being meaningfully realised
- hedonic well-being – form of well-being centred on pleasure, comfort and the presence of positive emotions
- eudaimonic well-being – form of well-being associated with meaning, personal growth and living according to one's values
- personal flourishing – state in which a person develops their abilities, maintains meaningful relationships and functions well across important areas of life
- intrinsic value – worth that something possesses in itself rather than because it produces another benefit
- emotional equilibrium – relatively stable emotional state in which positive and negative experiences remain manageable and balanced
- intrinsic motivation – desire to do something because the activity itself is satisfying or meaningful rather than because of external rewards
- value congruence – degree to which a person's behaviour and life choices correspond to their underlying values

# 6. Reading – Happiness Is Not the Whole Story

Read.

When people are asked what they want from life, happiness is one of the most common answers. Yet defining happiness is considerably more difficult than it initially appears. A pleasant emotional state can contribute to a good life, but few people would argue that pleasure alone is sufficient.

Consider activities that require considerable effort or sacrifice. Raising children, developing expertise, maintaining relationships and pursuing demanding goals can all involve frustration, anxiety and disappointment. Nevertheless, people frequently describe these experiences as among the most valuable aspects of their lives.

This distinction has led researchers and philosophers to differentiate between hedonic and eudaimonic well-being. The former emphasises positive emotion, pleasure and comfort. The latter concerns meaning, growth, purpose and the extent to which people feel that they are living in accordance with their values.

The two are not necessarily in conflict. A meaningful life can also be highly enjoyable. However, problems arise when happiness is interpreted as the constant presence of positive emotions and the absence of discomfort. Difficult experiences are not automatically evidence that something has gone wrong. In some circumstances, frustration, uncertainty and sacrifice may be inseparable from activities that contribute significantly to personal flourishing.

A good life may therefore be less about maximising happiness at every moment and more about creating a life that remains valuable when viewed as a whole.
