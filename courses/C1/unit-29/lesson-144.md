# Lesson 144 – Is More Always Better?

## 🎯 Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:

- discuss why having more does not always lead to greater satisfaction
- analyse the psychological effects of excessive choice and constant optimisation
- distinguish between maximising and satisficing
- evaluate the costs of continuously pursuing improvement
- use advanced vocabulary related to abundance, comparison and contentment

# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- maximisation tendency – inclination to search for the absolute best available option rather than accepting one that adequately satisfies one's needs
- satisficing – choosing an option that meets important requirements without continuing to search indefinitely for the theoretically best alternative
- choice overload – cognitive and emotional difficulty caused by having more options than can be comfortably evaluated
- opportunity cost – value of the alternative that is sacrificed when one option is chosen over another
- attentional scarcity – condition in which limited mental attention becomes overwhelmed by competing demands, information or possibilities
- perpetual dissatisfaction – persistent inability to feel satisfied because attention remains focused on what could still be improved or acquired
- sufficiency mindset – approach based on recognising when one's needs have been adequately met rather than continually seeking expansion
- aspirational consumption – acquisition of products or experiences associated with a desired lifestyle, identity or social position
- optimisation culture – social tendency to treat increasing numbers of areas of life as problems that should constantly be measured and improved
- marginal utility – additional benefit or satisfaction obtained from acquiring one more unit of something

# 6. Reading – When Improvement Becomes a Trap

Read.

The desire to improve our circumstances is hardly irrational. Better healthcare, greater financial security, technological progress and expanded personal freedom have transformed human lives. Yet the assumption that improvement must always mean "more" deserves closer examination.

One problem is diminishing marginal utility. The first major improvement in a particular area may dramatically increase well-being, while subsequent improvements produce progressively smaller benefits. Moving from an unsafe home to a comfortable one can fundamentally alter someone's life. Moving from a comfortable home to one with an additional unused bedroom is unlikely to produce an equivalent increase in satisfaction.

Abundance can also create new psychological costs. Greater choice increases the possibility of finding something well suited to our preferences, but it simultaneously increases the number of rejected alternatives. This makes opportunity costs more visible. Instead of evaluating whether our chosen option is good, we may compare it endlessly with everything we could have chosen instead.

A similar dynamic can emerge in personal development. Exercise, productivity, sleep, relationships and even leisure can become subjects of constant measurement and optimisation. Activities once valued for their own sake begin to feel like projects requiring continuous improvement.

The problem is not ambition itself. The ability to imagine better possibilities drives innovation and personal growth. The difficulty arises when improvement has no endpoint. If every achievement merely establishes a new baseline from which further optimisation must begin, satisfaction is permanently postponed.

Learning to recognise sufficiency may therefore be as important as learning to pursue improvement. "Enough" need not mean stagnation. Sometimes it simply means deciding that further gains are no longer worth what they would require us to sacrifice.
