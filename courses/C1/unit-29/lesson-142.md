# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- diminishing returns – principle whereby each additional increase in something produces a progressively smaller benefit
- relative deprivation – feeling disadvantaged because others appear to have more, even when one's own basic needs are adequately met
- hedonic adaptation – tendency to become accustomed to positive or negative changes and gradually return towards a relatively stable level of satisfaction
- status consumption – purchasing goods or services partly to demonstrate wealth, prestige or social position
- socioeconomic comparison – evaluation of one's financial or social position in relation to other people
- perceived affluence – subjective sense of being financially comfortable or wealthy, which may differ from one's actual income
- material aspiration – desire to acquire greater wealth, possessions or material comfort
- lifestyle inflation – tendency for spending and expectations to increase as income rises
- financial autonomy – ability to make important life choices without being severely constrained by financial dependence
- consumption externality – indirect effect that one person's consumption has on other people or society, including effects on status expectations, resources or the environment

# 6. Reading – How Much Is Enough?

Read.

Money clearly matters for well-being. A lack of sufficient income can create persistent stress, restrict access to housing and healthcare, reduce autonomy and make unexpected expenses extremely difficult to manage. In such circumstances, additional income can substantially improve quality of life.

The relationship becomes more complicated once basic needs and a reasonable degree of security have been achieved. Higher income can still provide benefits, but those benefits may produce diminishing returns. Moving from financial insecurity to stability can transform everyday life, whereas moving from an already comfortable lifestyle to a more luxurious one may have a considerably smaller psychological effect.

One explanation is hedonic adaptation. People rapidly become accustomed to improved circumstances. A larger home, a higher salary or a more expensive car may initially produce satisfaction, but eventually becomes part of ordinary life. Expectations can rise alongside income, creating a situation in which objectively wealthier individuals do not necessarily feel wealthier.

Comparison further complicates the picture. People rarely evaluate their financial position in complete isolation. Someone may feel prosperous among people earning less and relatively deprived among people earning considerably more. Consequently, the psychological value of money depends partly on what others possess and on what individuals believe they ought to possess.

The relevant question may therefore not be whether money can buy happiness, but which uses of money produce lasting improvements in well-being and when the pursuit of additional wealth begins to compete with other things people value.

# 8. What Would You Do With €1 Million?

You can spend a maximum of:

- €300,000 on housing
- €100,000 on possessions
- €150,000 on travel and experiences
- €150,000 on education or personal projects
- €200,000 on helping other people
- €300,000 on investments
- €200,000 on buying yourself more free time

You do not have to use the maximum in any category, but your total cannot exceed €1 million.

1. Which category received the most?
2. Which received nothing or very little?
3. Which expenditure would create immediate pleasure?
4. Which would probably improve long-term life satisfaction?
5. Would giving some of the money away improve or reduce your own well-being?
