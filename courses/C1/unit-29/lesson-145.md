# 3. Advanced Language

Work with these terms.

- psychological richness – quality of a life characterised by varied, complex and perspective-changing experiences rather than primarily by happiness or purpose
- experiential depth – degree to which experiences provoke substantial emotional, intellectual or personal engagement
- retrospective evaluation – judgement made about an experience or period of life after it has ended
- value congruence – extent to which a person's actions and decisions correspond to their deeply held principles
- purposeful sacrifice – deliberate acceptance of a cost, difficulty or loss in pursuit of something regarded as sufficiently important
- delayed gratification – willingness to give up an immediate reward in order to obtain a more valuable future benefit
- transformative experience – experience that significantly changes a person's preferences, identity or understanding of the world
- narrative coherence – extent to which different experiences can be understood as forming a meaningful and intelligible life story
- existential satisfaction – deep sense that one's life as a whole has been worthwhile or consistent with one's values
- counterfactual regret – regret arising from imagining how life might have developed if a different decision had been made

# 6. Reading – More Than Happiness

Read.

If people could design their ideal lives in advance, many would probably choose happiness, security and meaningful relationships. Few would deliberately request failure, confusion, uncertainty or loss. Yet when people look back on their lives, difficult experiences often occupy an unexpectedly important place in the stories they tell about themselves.

This does not mean that suffering is inherently beneficial. Many painful experiences cause damage without producing wisdom, strength or personal growth. Nevertheless, a life completely protected from difficulty might also lack some experiences capable of transforming how a person understands themselves and the world.

This possibility has contributed to interest in the concept of psychological richness. Unlike happiness, which emphasises positive emotion and satisfaction, or meaning, which is often associated with purpose and contribution, psychological richness concerns complexity, novelty and perspective change. An experience can therefore make someone's life psychologically richer without making them happier.

The distinction complicates the idea of an ideal life. A person might reasonably choose uncertainty because they value discovery, or accept temporary discomfort because an experience offers exceptional experiential depth. Another person might prefer stability and find no reason to seek disruption merely for its own sake. Neither choice necessarily represents a failure to live well.

Perhaps the difficulty lies in our desire to reduce a good life to a single measurement. Happiness asks whether life feels good. Meaning asks whether it matters. Psychological richness asks whether it is interesting, complex and capable of changing us. A life may contain all three, but the balance between them is unlikely to be identical for everyone.
