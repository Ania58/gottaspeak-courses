# 3. Language From the Topic

Work with these expressions.

- utilitarian reasoning – ethical approach that seeks the greatest overall benefit for the greatest number of people
- moral absolutism – belief that certain actions are always right or always wrong regardless of circumstances
- ethical trade-off – situation requiring the sacrifice of one important value to preserve another
- consequentialist perspective – judging actions primarily by their outcomes rather than intentions
- moral justification – explanation demonstrating why a difficult decision is ethically defensible
- competing interests – legitimate priorities that cannot all be satisfied simultaneously
- proportional harm – principle of comparing the amount of harm caused with the benefit achieved
- ethical consistency – applying the same moral standards across different situations
- personal autonomy – right of individuals to make decisions concerning their own lives
- irreversible consequence – outcome that cannot be undone once the decision has been made

# 6. Reading

Read.

Ethical dilemmas become particularly challenging when every available option results in some form of harm. In such situations, people often rely on different moral principles rather than objective facts alone. Some believe that the morally correct decision is the one producing the greatest overall benefit, while others argue that certain actions should never be performed, regardless of the outcome.

Research suggests that emotions also play an important role in ethical decision-making. Although people often believe they make rational decisions, empathy, fear, personal relationships and cultural values frequently influence moral judgement.

For this reason, ethical debates rarely produce universal agreement. Instead, they encourage individuals to examine their values, question their assumptions and recognise the complexity of difficult decisions.
