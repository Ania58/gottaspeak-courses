# 3. Language From the Topic

Work with these expressions.

- algorithmic accountability – principle that individuals or organisations should remain answerable for decisions produced by automated systems
- moral agency – capacity to understand moral consequences and make decisions based on ethical considerations
- accountability gap – situation in which it becomes unclear who is responsible for harm caused by an autonomous system
- human oversight – supervision of automated processes by people capable of reviewing or overriding decisions
- algorithmic opacity – difficulty in understanding how a complex automated system reached a particular conclusion
- value alignment – process of ensuring that an AI system behaves consistently with intended human values and priorities
- delegated decision-making – transfer of responsibility for making decisions to another person or system
- ethical safeguard – mechanism designed to prevent or reduce ethically unacceptable outcomes
- moral responsibility – obligation to answer for the ethical consequences of one's actions or decisions
- algorithmic discrimination – systematic disadvantage experienced by certain groups as a result of automated decision-making

# 6. Reading

Read.

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly involved in healthcare, recruitment, finance and transportation, an important ethical question emerges: should machines merely provide information, or should they be permitted to make decisions that significantly affect human lives?

Supporters of automated decision-making argue that algorithms can process far more information than humans and may be less vulnerable to fatigue, emotion and certain forms of personal prejudice. Yet algorithms are not inherently neutral. They are developed by humans, trained on existing data and designed according to particular objectives. Consequently, automated systems may reproduce biases already present in society.

An even more difficult problem concerns accountability. If an autonomous system causes harm, responsibility may be distributed among developers, companies, users and regulators. The greater the autonomy granted to machines, the harder it may become to determine who should ultimately be held accountable.

For this reason, some researchers argue that the central question is not whether AI can make decisions, but which decisions humans should be willing to delegate.

# 8. Set the Limits

Imagine that governments decide AI must be classified according to how much decision-making power it receives.

Create rules for these three levels.

### Level 1 – AI advises

The system provides information, but a human makes every final decision.

### Level 2 – AI decides with supervision

The system makes decisions automatically, but humans can intervene.

### Level 3 – AI decides independently

The system makes and executes decisions without prior human approval.

Now decide which level should be permitted in:

- healthcare
- recruitment
- banking
- policing
- education
- transport

Justify your choices.
