# 3. Language From the Topic

Work with these expressions.

- media literacy – ability to critically evaluate information presented through different media
- misinformation – false or inaccurate information shared without the intention to deceive
- disinformation – deliberately false information intended to mislead people
- source credibility – degree to which an information source can be trusted
- fact-checking – process of verifying whether information is accurate
- sensationalism – presentation designed to provoke strong emotional reactions rather than inform objectively
- algorithmic recommendation – automated selection of content based on user behaviour
- echo chamber – environment in which people mainly encounter opinions similar to their own
- confirmation bias – tendency to accept information that supports existing beliefs
- digital footprint – record of a person's online activity

# 6. Reading

Read.

The internet has transformed access to information. Within seconds, people can learn about scientific discoveries, world events and medical advice from thousands of different sources. However, the speed and volume of online information also make it easier for false claims to spread.

Not all inaccurate information is created intentionally. Misinformation is often shared by people who genuinely believe it is true. Disinformation, in contrast, is deliberately designed to deceive, influence public opinion or create confusion.

For this reason, media literacy has become an essential skill. Reliable readers compare multiple sources, verify evidence and remain willing to change their opinions when stronger evidence becomes available.
